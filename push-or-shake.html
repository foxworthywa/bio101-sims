BIO 101 · Chapter 4 · How ATP does work

Push or shake?

An engine works by pushing. A protein machine can't, because at its size everything is stuck in something like molasses while being shaken by an earthquake of jostling water molecules. These two simulations show how ATP powers a machine anyway.

1. The marble in the shaking tray

The curve is a tray with two dips. Each dip is one shape the pump can take: open to the inside or open to the outside. The marble shows which shape the pump is in right now.

  1. Press Attach phosphate. ATP never pushes the marble. It reshapes the tray so the other dip is deeper, and the shaking carries the marble over.
  2. Drag Shaking to zero, then attach or remove the phosphate. With no shaking, the marble stays stuck even when the other dip is deeper.
  3. Switch to Engine world and press Push the marble. With momentum and no molasses, a push sends it rolling back and forth. Try the same push in protein world.

2. The sodium–potassium pump at work

The same tray now runs the pump. The pump accepts a phosphate only when all three sodium ions are aboard, and lets it go only when both potassium ions are aboard. Each change in what is bound reshapes the tray.

  1. Turn off Sodium inside the cell. Does the pump keep using ATP?
  2. Turn sodium back on and turn off ATP supply. Where does the pump get stuck, and why?
  3. Set Shaking to zero. Plenty of sodium and ATP, yet the pump stalls. What does that tell you about where the motion comes from?
  4. Watch the counters. Each ATP should move 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in.

The takeaway: ATP does not shove the pump. Its phosphate changes which shape is most stable, the constant shaking of molecules carries the pump into that shape, and the ions that must be aboard at each step keep the cycle in order.
